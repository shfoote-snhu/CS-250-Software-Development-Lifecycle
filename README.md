# CS-250-Software-Development-Lifecycle
Sprint Review and Retrospective from CS 250
CS 250: Software Development Lifecycle

This repository holds my Sprint Review and Retrospective from CS 250 (Final Project). In this course, I worked as part of a Scrum team building the SNHU Travel app. The document looks back at how the team used Agile and Scrum. It covers each Scrum role, how we handled a change partway through the project, and how well the process worked overall.

How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How do user stories help?

The first step is to listen to the people who will use the program and learn what problem they need solved. A feature only helps if it meets a real need. User stories keep that need clear and in focus. A story like "As a traveler, I want to see the top destinations so that I can plan my trip" says who the user is, what they want, and why it matters. That makes it clear to the whole team what "done" should look like.

User stories also break a big project into smaller pieces that can be finished in one sprint. Each story gets acceptance criteria, so the developer knows what to build and the tester knows what to check. In this project, the Product Owner wrote and ordered the stories, and the team could ask questions before starting work. This made it much less likely that we would build the wrong thing.

How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I want to use in the future?

I like to build in small steps and get feedback early instead of trying to plan everything up front. This course showed me why that matters. When the client changed direction in the middle of the project, an Agile team can adjust in the next sprint. A Waterfall team might have had to throw away months of work.

Agile processes I want to keep using:

Sprint planning, so the team agrees on a small, clear goal for each sprint.
Daily stand-ups, which are short check-ins that bring problems to light early.
Backlog refinement, so the most important work is always at the top and ready to go.
Sprint reviews, to show working software and get real feedback from stakeholders.
Retrospectives, so the team keeps getting better at how it works, not just what it builds.
Test-driven development, writing tests first so I know what the code needs to do.
What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

A good team member communicates clearly and often. That means speaking up early about blockers instead of waiting until they turn into big problems. It also means being honest in stand-ups and retrospectives, even when things are not going well.

A good team member also respects the other roles on the team. The Product Owner, Scrum Master, developers, and testers each bring something different, and the project works best when everyone trusts each other. Finally, a good team member stays flexible. Requirements change, and the goal is to deliver value to the user, not to defend your own plan.
